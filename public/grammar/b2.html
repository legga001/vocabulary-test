Reason about possibilities

  • Third conditional

    Use if plus had and a past participle for an imagined change to the past. Use would have plus a past participle for its imagined result.

    In action
    If we’d left earlier, we would’ve caught the train.

  • Mixed conditionals

    Mix the time frames when the condition and result belong to different times. A past change can have an imagined result now.

    In action
    If I’d charged my phone last night, I’d have a working map now.

  • Past deduction

    Use must have for a strong inference, might have for a possibility and can’t have for a past event you think was impossible. Follow each with a past participle.

    In action
    The gate is open. Someone must have unlocked it. It can’t have been Jo. She’s away.

  • Past advice and missed opportunities

    Use should have to talk about a better past action. Could have can describe an opportunity someone had but didn’t take. Needn’t have means the action happened and was unnecessary.

    In action
    I should have packed a coat. I could have borrowed one. You needn’t have bought me a new one!

  • Wish, if only and would rather

    Use wish plus a past form for a present situation you want to change, or wish plus the past perfect for a regret. After I’d rather plus a different subject, use a past form for a preference about now or later.

    In action
    I wish I had more time. If only I’d started earlier! I’d rather you waited outside.

Shape and report information

  • Non-defining relative clauses

    Use commas around extra information. Use who, which or whose, and keep the relative word in the clause. Use which to refer back to a whole idea.

    In action
    Mia, whose bike is red, led the way. We missed the bus, which was annoying.

  • Passive across tenses

    Keep the event’s tense in the forms before the past participle. Use being for an action in progress, been after have, and be after a modal.

    In action
    The pitch is being repaired. The lights have been replaced. The match may be postponed.

  • Have or get something done

    Use have or get plus an object and a past participle when you arrange for someone to do something for you.

    In action
    I’m having my bike repaired. I got my hair cut yesterday.

  • Reporting verbs and their patterns

    Choose a verb that conveys the speaker’s intent. A verb such as advise takes a person plus to, admit can take -ing, and promise can take to.

    In action
    She advised me to wait. He admitted forgetting the key. He promised to bring it tomorrow.

  • Gerunds and infinitives with a change of meaning

    Some verbs can take either form, with different meanings. Stop doing means end the action. Stop to do means pause one action in order to do another.

    In action
    I stopped running because I was tired. I stopped to tie my laces.

  • Future continuous and future perfect

    Use will be plus -ing for an action in progress at a future time. Use will have plus a past participle for something completed by a future deadline.

    In action
    At nine I’ll be travelling. By noon I’ll have arrived.

  • Concession and contrast

    Use although or even though with a clause. Use despite or in spite of with a noun or an -ing form. Use however to connect contrasting statements.

    In action
    Although it rained, we played. Despite the rain, we played.

  • Articles and generalisations

    Use plural or uncountable nouns without the when talking generally. Use the for a specific group or amount the reader can identify.

    In action
    Foxes often hunt at night. The foxes behind our house are surprisingly noisy.

Quick check

If I’d saved the file, I ___ my work. (wouldn’t lose / wouldn’t have lost)

Reveal the answer

Use wouldn’t have lost for an imagined result in the past.
