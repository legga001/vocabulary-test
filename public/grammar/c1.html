Put emphasis where you want it

  • Negative inversion

    When a negative or limiting expression opens a sentence, put the auxiliary before the subject. Add do, does or did if the original sentence has no auxiliary. This can sound formal or dramatic.

    In action
    Rarely do we see a fox in daylight. Only then did I realise I’d lost my key.

  • Cleft sentences

    Use it was … that or what … is to put a particular detail under the spotlight. The rest of the sentence supplies the context.

    In action
    It was Jo who found the key. What I need is a good night’s sleep.

  • Inverted conditionals

    In formal writing, omit if and place had, were or should before the subject in certain conditionals. The time and meaning of the condition stay the same.

    In action
    Had I known, I’d have called. Were the plan to change, we’d let you know.

  • Fronting and information flow

    Move a phrase to the beginning to connect it to the previous sentence or highlight a contrast. Keep the usual word order after many fronted objects in ordinary prose.

    In action
    That part I understood. The rest was a mystery.

  • Substitution and ellipsis

    Avoid repeating words that are already clear. So can stand in for a clause, one can replace a countable noun, and an auxiliary can stand in for a verb phrase.

    In action
    Will it rain? I think so. Maya can come, and Jo can too.

Write with range and control

  • Participle clauses

    An -ing clause can add an action or reason. Having plus a past participle signals an earlier action. The understood subject should match the subject of the main clause.

    In action
    Having checked the map, we took the next turn. Waiting outside, I heard the bell.

  • Reduced relative clauses

    Use an -ing form for many active descriptions and a past participle for many passive ones. Attach the phrase directly to the noun it describes.

    In action
    The fans waiting outside have tickets. The tickets printed yesterday have the wrong date.

  • Formal recommendations and the subjunctive

    After verbs such as recommend or insist, a that-clause can use the base verb for a requested action. British English also commonly uses should plus the base verb.

    In action
    The coach insisted that everyone be on time. We recommend that applicants should apply early.

  • Reporting passives

    Use it is said that or someone is said to when the source is unspecified. Use to have plus a past participle when the reported event happened earlier.

    In action
    It is believed that the bridge is safe. The fox is thought to have escaped through a gap.

  • Hedging claims

    Use modals and phrases such as appears to or is likely to to signal how much certainty your evidence supports. Different choices imply different levels of confidence.

    In action
    The delay may have affected the result. The delay is likely to have affected the result.

  • Nominalisation

    Turn an action into a noun phrase to make it the topic of a sentence. Name the person doing the action when that information matters to your reader.

    In action
    The council approved the plan. The council’s approval allowed work to begin.

  • Complex noun phrases

    Put key information near the noun it describes. A following phrase can narrow the meaning without piling several modifiers in front of the noun.

    In action
    The report on flood risks in coastal towns arrives on Friday.

  • Advanced linking and register

    Use whereas to set two clauses against each other. Use nevertheless to signal an unexpected continuation in formal writing. In a casual message, still often sounds more natural.

    In action
    The trail was steep. Nevertheless, we reached the top before noon. → It was steep. Still, we made it before noon.

Quick check

Only then ___ we notice the fox. (did / had)

Reveal the answer

Use did before the subject. Notice stays in the base form.
