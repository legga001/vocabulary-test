Tell stories and connect time

  • Present perfect with for and since

    Use the present perfect for a situation that started in the past and continues now. For gives the length of time. Since gives the starting point.

  • Present perfect continuous

    Use have or has been plus -ing to emphasise an activity or its duration. The simple form often emphasises a completed result.

  • Past perfect

    Use had plus a past participle to show that something happened before another point in the past, especially when the order needs clarifying.

  • Used to

    Use used to plus the base verb for past habits or states that have changed. In a question with did, write use to.

  • Temporal clauses

    After when, as soon as or until, use the present simple in clauses about many future situations. The main clause can use will.

Explain what might happen

  • Zero and first conditionals

    For general results, use the present simple in both clauses. For a real future possibility, use if plus the present simple, then will plus the base verb for the result.

  • Second conditional

    Picture an unlikely or imaginary situation with if plus a past form, then would plus the base verb for the result. If I were you is a common way to give advice.

  • May, might and could

    Use these modals plus the base verb for possibilities. A time expression can make it clear whether you mean now or later.

  • Defining relative clauses

    Use who for people and which for things. That can often replace either in a defining clause. Leave the relative word out when it is the object.

  • Present and past passive

    Use a form of be plus a past participle to focus on what receives the action. Mention the person doing it with by if it matters.

  • Reported statements

    Use say or tell to pass on a message. Tell usually takes a person as its object. After a past reporting verb, present forms often shift into the past, though an idea that is still true can stay in the present.

  • Indirect questions

    After a polite opening such as Could you tell me, use statement word order. Use if or whether for a yes-or-no question.

  • Question tags

    Use a short question at the end to check or invite agreement. A positive statement usually takes a negative tag, and a negative statement takes a positive one.

Quick check

If the rain stops, we ___ outside. (play / will play)

Reveal the answer

Use will play for the result of this real future possibility.
